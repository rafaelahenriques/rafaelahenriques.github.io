# The blank editor cursor: Getting over the fear of starting

There is a specific feeling every developer knows. You’ve just run `npm create astro@latest`, the dependencies are installed, and you open `index.astro`. The cursor blinks. And you freeze.

Suddenly, the internal monologue kicks in. *“Why is this file structure different? What is hydration? Everyone on Twitter seems to know this already. I should probably just go back to what I already know.”*

Starting new things is terrifying, whether it's a new tech stack, writing a blog post, or picking up a new hobby. The friction isn't usually the difficulty of the task itself—it's the fear of the unknown.

Here is the framework I use to get past that initial wall of fear and paralysis.

## 1. Embrace the "Valley of Stupid"
First, let’s reframe the fear. When you feel incompetent at the start of a project, that is actually a good sign. 

If you never feel "stupid," you are likely staying within your comfort zone, solving problems you already know the answers to. Growth only happens when you risk looking foolish. In educational psychology, this is often linked to the *Zone of Proximal Development*—the space where a task is just hard enough to be challenging, but not impossible.

**Remember:** Every Senior Engineer you admire once stared at a screen, totally baffled by a simple error message. The difference is they didn't quit when they felt stupid. They Googled it.

## 2. Escape "Tutorial Hell"
A common way we procrastinate starting is by "learning." We watch 10 hours of YouTube tutorials on how to build a clone of Netflix. We nod along, feeling productive.

But watching someone else code is like watching someone else work out. *They* get stronger; you just get entertainment.

To break the fear of starting, you have to break the tutorial:
1.  **Pause the video.**
2.  **Type the code.**
3.  **Change it intentionally.** Make the button blue instead of red. Break the layout. See what happens.

Real learning begins when the tutorial ends and you are left alone with the documentation.

## 3. Lower the Stakes (The MVP Mindset)
The biggest killer of new projects is High Expectations. We imagine the final, polished product (a portfolio that wins Awwwards) before we’ve even written a line of HTML. This creates a gap between where we are and where we want to be that feels impossible to cross.

**Solution:** Aim for "trash."
Seriously. Give yourself permission to write bad code. 
* Don't worry about the folder structure yet.
* Don't worry about DRY (Don't Repeat Yourself) principles.
* Just make something appear on the screen.

Adopt the mantra used by Kent Beck:
> **"Make it work, make it right, make it fast."**

You can refactor bad code. You cannot refactor a blank page.

## 4. Use Time Boxing to trick your brain
When a task feels overwhelming ("Build a Portfolio"), your brain perceives it as a threat and triggers an avoidance response. This is why you suddenly feel the urge to clean your room instead of coding.

To bypass this, I use **Time Boxing**. I tell myself:
*"I am not going to build the website today. I am just going to spend 20 minutes finding a font I like."*

Almost always, once I break that static friction of starting, the momentum carries me for an hour or more. The hardest part of going for a run is putting on your shoes; the hardest part of coding is opening the terminal.

## 5. The Comparison Trap
If you are building a portfolio, it is easy to look at other developers with 10 years of experience and feel inadequate. 

You are comparing your **Chapter 1** to their **Chapter 20**.

When you look at a polished GitHub repository, you don't see the 50 previous versions that were messy, buggy, and unoptimized. You don't see the hours they spent debugging a missing semicolon. Focus on your own velocity, not their position.

## 6. The Confidence Loop
Many people wait until they feel confident to start coding. But confidence is not a prerequisite for action; it is a **result** of action.

$$
\text{Exposure} + \text{Repetition} = \text{Competence} \rightarrow \text{Confidence}
$$

1.  **Exposure:** Try the thing (and likely fail).
2.  **Repetition:** Try it again (fail a little less).
3.  **Competence:** Suddenly, you understand it.
4.  **Confidence:** Now you feel ready.

## Final Thoughts: Permissionless Leverage
You don’t need a degree, a certification, or permission to start building. The internet is the ultimate permissionless environment. No one is going to tap you on the shoulder and say, *"Okay, you are now allowed to call yourself a developer."*

If you are reading this and have a project sitting in your "Ideas" folder, this is your sign. 

Open the terminal. Run the build command. **Make a mess.**
